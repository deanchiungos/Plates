# Plate design history — scraped from Wikipedia

`plate-history.csv` is generated by `ios/tools/wiki_plates.py`. **4,305 designs across
all 65 jurisdictions — 50 states, DC, Puerto Rico and the 13 provinces and territories
— spanning 1903 to 2026.** Every row is one passenger baseplate: image, first year
issued, description, and the image's licence resolved from Commons.

Re-run the script rather than editing the CSV. Article wikitext and licence lookups are
both cached under `.wiki-cache/`; delete it to re-fetch.

## Why this replaced the Keegan catalogue

| | Keegan | Wikipedia |
| --- | --- | --- |
| designs | 75 usable | 4,305 |
| historical | none | back to 1903 |
| states missing their base design | 14 | 0 |
| Canada and PR | absent | 14 jurisdictions, 528 designs |
| licence | none stated on anything | explicit per image |

Coverage runs from Nunavut's 6 designs (it has only existed since 1999) to Wisconsin's
103. Median 52. 3,115 of 4,305 rows resolve to a picture; the other 1,190 are designs
Wikipedia documents but does not illustrate, and are kept as rows so the gaps stay
countable.

## Licensing: the column describes the photograph, not the design

**Correcting an earlier reading of this data.** I first reported the 9 `Fair use` rows as
"unusable in a shipped app". That framing was wrong, and the file pages say so plainly.

All eight Nebraska files carry the same line in their non-free rationale:

> "Copyrighted material in question is the plate design, not the photo of the plate."

with `Immediate source: License plate in personal collection`. So the photograph is a
Wikipedian's own shot of a plate they own, and the non-free tag records **that
uploader's opinion about the plate design**, not any claim over the photo.

Meanwhile the 2,447 CC BY-SA and 585 PD/CC0 rows are also photographs of plates, taken by
other contributors who reached the *opposite* conclusion about the identical question
and tagged them `PD-textlogo` — design below the threshold of originality. This is
inconsistent tagging practice across Wikipedia, not a difference in the underlying facts.

Two consequences:

**The photo licence and the design question are independent.** Filtering out `Fair use`
rows and treating `Public domain` rows as safe is a category error: the design question
is identical for all 3,115 photographs in the set. What actually varies is whether you owe the
photographer a credit line.

| photo licence | rows | what you owe the photographer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CC BY-SA | 2,447 | credit + share-alike on the image |
| PD / CC0 / WTFPL | 585 | nothing |
| CC BY | 43 | credit |
| other free (OGL-C, no restrictions) | 2 | check the file page |
| unresolved | 12 | check the file page |
| tagged non-free | 9 | see above — 8 are one uploader's view of the *design* |

**Ohio 2009 is the one real exception.** Its source is `Ohio Bureau of Motor Vehicles`
— the state's own stock artwork, not a contributor's photograph. That is a genuinely
different situation from the Nebraska eight and should be treated separately.

Commons contributors are not courts, in either direction. `PD-textlogo` is no more
authoritative than `Non-free`; both are volunteer judgement calls about whether a state
plate clears the originality threshold. Not legal advice.

## Current-design coverage

Reconciled by hand, jurisdiction by jurisdiction. All 65 resolve to an identified current
design. **60 have a photograph of it and 5 do not — AZ, KS, SC, WY, YT.** Counting rows
rather than jurisdictions, 123 designs are in issue somewhere and 27 of them have no
picture, because several jurisdictions issue more than one plate at a time.

An earlier version of this file said all 65 were covered. That was wrong, and the way it
was wrong is the subject of the next section.

Five parser faults were hiding the truth, all found by checking the anomalies rather
than trusting the totals:

**`WANTED_SECTION` matched "Non-passenger plates".** The pattern searched anywhere in a
heading, so a heading most of these articles carry pulled in every trailer, motorcycle,
dealer, OHV and U.S. Senate table alongside the passenger series. That was the 8.4%
contamination, and worse, for eight jurisdictions it buried the real current-design row
under a trailer plate. A negative lookbehind fixed it: **279 stray rows → 13**.

**De-duplication collapsed the placeholder rows.** Rows were de-duplicated on image
filename, and `Blank License Plate Shape.svg` is *one file reused across every gap in an
article* — so every placeholder row after the first was discarded as a duplicate. That
silently deleted the current designs of Alaska, Idaho, Missouri and Utah, all of which
are "– present" rows illustrated with it. Restoring them added **957 rows**.

**The date column was found by search rather than by position.** "First short cell
containing a year" read Delaware's serial range *"2001 to approximately 2500"* as a date
range. Delaware correctly resolves to its 1969 design — which it really does still issue.

**Columns were guessed at instead of read.** Reading the date as "the cell after the
image" and the design as "the first long cell" works until a table has a Slogan column,
or a Serials issued cell longer than the description, or any `rowspan` shifting the
columns along. Missouri's current row came out described by its *slogan*; the serial
format was landing in the description often enough to be a documented caveat. The parser
now reads the header row and takes columns by name — Image, Dates issued / First issued,
Design / Description — and expands every `rowspan` into the rows it covers before
looking at anything. 80 tables, 78 with usable headers; the two without fall back to the
old scanner. The "description is sometimes a serial range" caveat is gone: **0 rows.**

**A picture was filled in from anywhere rather than from the row above.** See below.

Seven jurisdictions have an open-ended final row: a start date and no end, so nothing in
the row says "present" even though it is the plate in issue. Each was checked
individually and is listed with its reason in `LAST_ROW_IS_CURRENT` — CA, DE, IL, IN,
NJ, NU, YT.

## Showing the wrong plate, which is worse than showing none

Reported from the browser: California's card for **2026** showed the 1982–87 sunset
plate. Not a near miss — a different plate, forty years older, on the row a spotter would
most likely look at.

Two bugs met there, and each was doing damage on its own.

### Wikipedia says "same design as above" and the parser was not listening

California's 2026 row is a serial-format change — 1ABC123 exhausted, 123ABC1 begins —
sitting inside a Design cell that spans six rows back to 1993. The encyclopedia is
stating outright that the plate is unchanged. The row has no picture of its own, so it
read as a gap.

There are two ways these articles say it, and the parser now honours both:

| | rows resolved | what it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a spanned Design cell | 33 | one Design cell covers several rows |
| `"As above, but with…"` | 23 | the description names the row above |

Both borrow the picture from the row that owns it, and both write a sentence onto the
card saying which row it came from. **Never chained.** Idaho's current design is the
fourth link in an "as above" chain and Arizona's the third; following a chain to its end
hands Arizona's 2020 plate the 1996 embossed one, which looks nothing like it. One hop
from a row that genuinely holds a picture, or nothing.

This alone resolved AK, AR, CA, MO, NC, UT and NV from the article itself.

### `merge_image_sources.py` trusted an HTTP 200

The gaps were being filled from `current-plate-sources.csv` — state agencies, Keegan's
catalogue, Commons files the articles never linked. The source file recorded one image
per jurisdiction and the merge pasted it onto *any* current row lacking a picture. Every
note in that file read `verified HTTP 200`, which verifies that a URL loads and nothing
whatever about what is in the picture.

Every source has now been opened and looked at next to the row it was filling. Four were
a different plate:

| | what was merged | what the row actually is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **CA** | a 154×78 DMV gif of the 1982–87 sunset plate | the 2011 plate |
| **WY** | WYDOT's `2017 SAMPLE.jpg` — gold rope border, Green River Lakes | the design that replaced it: navy state flag, red and white border |
| **SC** | the `In God We Trust` base | Jan 2026, "Where the Revolutionary War Was Won" |
| **KS** | the state-seal / `AD ASTRA PER ASPERA` base | Aug 2024, gold gradient, state-shaped border, Capitol |

Missouri looked like a fifth and is not: Keegan's image is the `MISSOURI BICENTENNIAL
1821–2021` legend on the red-waves base, and Wikipedia illustrates the same base with a
bicentennial plate too. Missouri's fault was the column one — the old parser read its
*slogan* as the design, so the card said "SHOW-ME STATE centered at top".

Arizona's was rejected separately: it is a 4160×1920 photograph of the back of a Ford
Expedition, plate a few percent of the frame. It had passed an aspect-ratio check, which
is not a test of whether a picture is of a plate.

And where a jurisdiction issues several plates concurrently, one image was pasted across
all of them — Alaska's plain gold Gold Rush plate onto the August 2024 fireweed-and-Denali
row, North Carolina's `FIRST IN FREEDOM` onto the `IN GOD WE TRUST` row, Kentucky's 2021
base onto the 2020 one, New Hampshire's onto its veteran and National Guard plates.

The source file now carries a **`depicts_dates`** column naming the exact row an image
belongs to, and the merge refuses anything that does not match a row's `dates_issued`. A
source matching nothing prints `UNMATCHED SOURCE` rather than going quiet. Six survive:
AL, ID, IN, KY, NC, NE — each with what was actually seen in the picture written into its
note, rather than a status code.

### One image cell, several photographs

Reported from the browser: Montana's current plate was the pre-2016 font. 75 rows stack
more than one photograph in a single Image cell, and taking the first is usually right —
they are variants of one design, listed oldest first. On a row still being issued it is
the wrong end of the list. Montana's 2010–present cell holds `Montana 2010–2016 standard
license plate (old font).png` beside `Montana 2010-present standard plate (new font).jpg`,
and Montana changed the serial font in mid-2016.

The pick now keys on the filename saying outright which era it is — "present" wins, an
already-closed year range loses, everything else keeps its position. Deliberately narrow:
the only other current row that stacks images is Montana's 2012 replica pair, where
neither name distinguishes them, and that one is left alone.

Separately, a cell that holds the blank placeholder *and* a real photograph now shows the
photograph. That was 18 more rows illustrated, in AR, IL, KY, LA, ME, MN, NE, RI, TX and WI.

### An optional plate wearing a baseplate's dates

Also reported from the browser: Montana's 2012–present block listed the **Montana
Centennial** plate. Its Design cell reads *"As 1987-89 Montana Centennial plate (see
Optional Plates below), but with serial screened"* — the article pointing at its own
Optional Plates section, which is Wikipedia saying outright that this one is not a
standard issue. Montana's 2012 block is a set of replicas a motorist may choose between,
and the Centennial is the member of that set that does not belong in a baseplate history.

Rows whose Design cell cross-references an optional or specialty section are now dropped.
Keyed on the cross-reference rather than on words like "centennial" or "veteran", and it
matches **exactly one row** in the whole corpus — which is the point: the article said it,
so there is nothing to second-guess.

### Jurisdictions with no photograph of their current plate

**AZ, KS, SC, WY, YT.** Four of those had a picture before this pass and it was the wrong
plate. No replacement was found for them on Commons or on the agency sites — that is open
work, and a `?` is the correct thing to show until it is done.

Note that several jurisdictions legitimately have **more than one** current design — New
Hampshire ten concurrent "– present" rows, North Carolina three, Utah three, Alaska three.
"The current plate" is not always singular. New Hampshire's ten include veteran, disabled
veteran, National Guard and state-parks plates, which the article files inside the
passenger section; those are contamination and are still counted here.

## Street legal, which is a different question from "current"

For a spotting game the useful question is not when a design was issued but whether you
could see it on a road today. Sorted by year those look the same: a 1911 Alabama plate is
a museum piece, a **1963 California plate is still legal on the freeway**, because
California never made anyone hand them back.

### Cutoff years

`plate-validity-cutoffs.csv` holds a per-jurisdiction cutoff, and everything issued from
that year forward inherits it. **29 of 65 jurisdictions have one.** Two bases, kept
separate because the difference is real:

| basis | count | what it is | resolves to |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `stated` | 7 | an explicit sentence in the article | `valid` |
| `inferred` | 22 | the earliest design the still-valid table names, filled forward | `likely` |

Stated: **AK 1981, CA 1963, MD 1986, NJ 1959, NL 1982, ON 1973, OR 1955.** Each quoted in
the `evidence` column, because the qualifications matter — California's holds only "on
the vehicle to which they were originally issued and the vehicle has been continuously
registered", which is not a plain yes.

Inferred cutoffs assume the still-valid range runs contiguously from that year. That is
how states generally work, but it is an assumption rather than a quotation, so those rows
are marked **`likely`, never `valid`**. Iowa was excluded: its Status cell is a misparse,
not a validity statement.

| | rows |
| --- | --- |
| `current` — issued today | 123 |
| `valid` — documented road-legal | 118 |
| `likely` — after an inferred cutoff | 53 |
| `expiring` — being phased out | 6 |
| `unknown` — no evidence | 4,005 |

**301 designs have some evidence of being on the road today**; 248 of those are
documented rather than inferred.

### Nothing is assumed without evidence

**36 of 65 jurisdictions have no cutoff and no per-design statement.** For those, only the
present plate reads as on-the-road and nothing at all is claimed about their retired
designs. Wyoming is the clean case: 72 designs, exactly one road-legal — *June 2024 –
present*. Vermont likewise: 87 designs, one.

`unknown` means undocumented, never expired. No row is marked `valid` or `likely` without
a `validity_note` naming its source — verified mechanically at zero exceptions.

## Known data problems

- **3 red links.** Files referenced in Wikipedia tables that exist on neither Commons
  nor en.wiki. Faithfully scraped; broken upstream.
- **986 placeholder rows** carry Wikipedia's blank plate outline instead of a photo,
  and 204 more have no image cell at all — 1,190 designs the encyclopedia describes but
  does not illustrate. They are kept as rows so that the gaps are countable, and the
  browser draws them as `?`. This is the number an image-submission queue works from.
- **A handful of rows are not passenger baseplates** — mostly New Hampshire's veteran,
  National Guard and state-parks plates, which its article files inside the passenger
  section. Down from 279 once `WANTED_SECTION` stopped matching "Non-passenger plates",
  but not zero, and NH's ten concurrent "current" rows are the visible symptom.

## Parsing caveats

- Columns are read by header name, and `rowspan` is expanded before anything is read, so
  the old "description sometimes comes out as a serial range" caveat is gone. Two tables
  in the whole corpus have no header row and fall back to positional scanning.
- 56 rows show a picture borrowed from a neighbouring row, on Wikipedia's own statement
  that the design is shared. Each says so on its card, and `image_note` carries the
  sentence. Borrowing never chains.
- Renewal tabs, stickers and decals are filtered out by filename; they appear in the
  same tables in some states but are not plates.
- Georgia and Washington needed qualified article titles (`Georgia (U.S. state)`,
  `Washington (state)`) — the unqualified ones are disambiguation pages. The script now
  detects a disambiguation page and says so rather than silently returning nothing.
- Wikipedia returns 429 if you hammer the API. The 1.5s throttle is not optional.
